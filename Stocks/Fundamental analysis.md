# **How to Read an Annual Report**

## **1. Objective**

An annual report helps investors understand a company’s:

- Business model and products.
- Growth opportunities and business strategy.
- Financial performance.
- Debt and financial stability.
- Cash generation.
- Profitability.
- Management quality and corporate governance.
- Risks and future prospects.
- Fair value compared with its current stock price.

**Goal:** Determine whether the business is financially healthy, capable of growing, and available at a reasonable valuation.

An excellent company is not automatically an excellent investment. The price you pay matters.

---

# **2. How to Read an Annual Report**

You do not need to read every page of an annual report with equal attention.

Use the following sequence.

## **Step 1: Management Discussion and Analysis (MD&A)**

**Purpose:** Understand the business before looking at the numbers.

Focus on these sections:

### **A. Business overview**

Understand:

- What products or services does the company sell?
- Who are its customers?
- How does it generate revenue?
- Which geographical markets does it operate in?
- What are its major business segments?
- What differentiates it from competitors?

Example: For Bajaj Auto, investigate its two-wheelers, three-wheelers, electric vehicles, exports, and major brands.

### **B. Business strategy**

Look for:

- Expansion into new markets.
- New product launches.
- Electric vehicle strategy.
- Capacity expansion.
- Digital transformation.
- Cost reduction.
- Acquisitions and partnerships.
- Research and development investments.

Ask:

1. How will the company increase revenue?
2. How will it improve profitability?
3. Are its growth plans realistic?
4. What investments are required to achieve them?

### **C. Growth factors**

Potential growth drivers include:

- Increasing customer demand.
- Higher sales volumes.
- Premium products with better margins.
- International expansion.
- New product categories.
- Improved distribution networks.
- Technological innovation.
- Favorable industry trends.

**Important:** Separate actual growth drivers from management’s expectations. Promised growth is not guaranteed growth.

### **D. Risks**

Identify the company’s major business risks.

Examples:

- Increasing raw material prices.
- Competition from other manufacturers.
- Economic slowdown.
- Regulatory changes.
- Foreign exchange fluctuations.
- Supply chain disruptions.
- Technological changes.
- High dependence on a particular product or market.

Check whether the company explains how it plans to manage these risks.

### **E. Research and development (R&D)**

Investigate:

- R&D expenditure.
- New products under development.
- Patents and technological capabilities.
- Investments in electric vehicles or other emerging technologies.
- Whether R&D spending is producing commercially successful products.

High R&D spending is not automatically good. Evaluate whether it can lead to future revenue, competitive advantages, or cost savings.

### **F. Subsidiaries and associates**

Understand:

- What each important subsidiary does.
- Whether it contributes meaningfully to revenue or profits.
- Whether it is growing or losing money.
- Whether the parent company provides financial support to it.
- Whether the consolidated financial statements include its performance.

Also examine major related-party transactions where relevant.

### **Questions to answer after reading MD&A**

- Do I understand how this company makes money?
- What are its main growth drivers?
- What could go wrong?
- Does the company have a competitive advantage?
- What investments must it make to grow?

---

## **Step 2: Shareholder Information**

Use the shareholder section to understand ownership and potential governance risks.

Focus on:

- Promoter shareholding.
- Foreign institutional investor (FII/FPI) ownership.
- Domestic institutional investor (DII) ownership.
- Public shareholding.
- Promoter share pledging.
- Significant changes in ownership.
- Related-party transactions.

**Important:** Shareholding information is not useless.

It is less important than financial performance when evaluating the business itself, but it can reveal risks.

For example:

- Rising promoter ownership may indicate increased commitment, but it is not proof of business quality.
- High promoter pledging can create financial and governance risks.
- Sudden ownership changes may warrant investigation.

Treat ownership as supporting evidence rather than the primary reason to buy a stock.

---

## **Step 3: Corporate Governance**

Check:

- Auditor qualifications or concerns.
- Internal control weaknesses.
- Significant legal disputes.
- Related-party transactions.
- Director independence.
- Executive remuneration.
- Regulatory investigations.
- Accounting irregularities.
- Material changes in auditors or key executives.

Pay special attention to the auditor’s report and any qualifications in the financial statements.

A profitable company with weak governance can still be a poor investment.

---

## **Step 4: Directors’ Report**

You do not need to read the entire Directors’ Report line by line during your initial screening.

However, do not skip it completely.

Check for:

- Dividend recommendations.
- Major corporate developments.
- Changes in directors or key management.
- Significant legal or regulatory matters.
- Material events after the financial year.
- Important disclosures about the business.

Read the detailed sections when something requires investigation.

---

# **3. Consolidated vs. Standalone Financial Statements**

This distinction is essential.

## **Standalone financial statements**

These show the financial position and performance of the parent company itself.

## **Consolidated financial statements**

These combine the parent company and its subsidiaries, with appropriate adjustments and eliminations.

They generally provide a broader picture of the business group.

### **Which should I use?**

**Use consolidated financial statements as your starting point when analyzing a group with significant subsidiaries.**

However, also inspect standalone statements when necessary.

Standalone statements can help you understand:

- The parent’s own debt.
- Dividends received from subsidiaries.
- Loans and investments in subsidiaries.
- The parent’s ability to meet its own obligations.

Consolidated statements can help you understand:

- Group-wide revenue.
- Total group profitability.
- Group debt and assets.
- Cash generation across the group.

For associates and joint ventures, accounting treatment differs from that of fully consolidated subsidiaries.

**Rule:** Do not automatically assume that consolidated revenue, profit, or cash is entirely available to the parent company’s shareholders.

---

# **4. The Three Main Financial Statements**

An annual report contains three major financial statements.

|**Financial statement**|**Main question**|**What it tells you**|
|---|---|---|
|Profit and Loss Statement|Is the company profitable?|Revenue, expenses, and profit over a period|
|Balance Sheet|What does the company own and owe?|Assets, liabilities, and equity at a particular date|
|Cash Flow Statement|Is the company generating cash?|Cash inflows and outflows during a period|

Read all three together.

A company can report profits but still experience cash flow problems.

---

# **5. Profit and Loss Statement (P&L)**

## **5.1 What is it?**

The Profit and Loss Statement shows the company’s financial performance over a period, usually a financial year.

It tells you:

- How much revenue the company generated.
- How much it spent.
- How much profit it earned.
- How much tax it paid or incurred.

## **5.2 Revenue from operations**

Revenue from operations is the income generated by the company’s ordinary business activities.

Examples:

- Vehicle sales for an automobile manufacturer.
- Product sales for a consumer goods company.
- Subscription fees for a software company.

Revenue is often called the **top line**.

### **Questions to ask**

1. Is revenue increasing year over year?
2. Is growth driven by higher sales volume, higher prices, or both?
3. Is growth faster or slower than the industry?
4. Is revenue growth supported by cash collection?
5. Is the company becoming dependent on a single product or market?

Revenue growth alone does not prove that a company is becoming more profitable.

## **5.3 Other income**

Other income may include:

- Interest income.
- Investment income.
- Certain gains on asset sales.
- Other income outside ordinary operations.

Its composition depends on the company’s circumstances and accounting disclosures.

### **Important distinction**

**Total income = Revenue from operations + Other income**

Total income is not always the same as revenue from operations.

When evaluating the underlying business, distinguish operating revenue from other income.

## **5.4 Expenses**

Expenses can include:

- Raw materials consumed.
- Purchases of stock-in-trade.
- Changes in inventories.
- Employee benefit expenses.
- Finance costs.
- Depreciation and amortisation.
- Advertising and selling expenses.
- Rent and other operating expenses.
- Research and development expenses, where separately disclosed.

Inspect the notes to accounts for the composition of significant expense categories.

### **Questions to ask**

- Are expenses growing faster than revenue?
- Are raw material costs increasing?
- Is employee expenditure rising because of expansion?
- Are finance costs increasing?
- Is depreciation rising after major capital investments?
- Are exceptional or unusual expenses affecting reported profit?

## **5.5 Operating profit**

Operating profit measures the profitability of the company’s core business.

For a simplified analysis:

Operating profit = Revenue from operations − Operating expenses

However, the exact calculation depends on which expenses are included and whether the measure is adjusted.

A common analytical measure is EBITDA.

### **EBITDA**

EBITDA means:

**Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation and Amortisation**

It measures earnings before financing costs, taxes, and depreciation and amortisation.

A simplified formula is:

EBITDA = Operating revenue − Operating expenses excluding depreciation and amortisation

The exact calculation should be reconciled with the company’s reported figures.

EBITDA is not the same as cash flow. It does not account for capital expenditure, working capital changes, interest payments, or taxes.

## **5.6 EBIT**

EBIT means Earnings Before Interest and Taxes.

A simplified relationship is:

EBIT = EBITDA − Depreciation and Amortisation

EBIT reflects the operating earnings remaining after depreciation and amortisation.

## **5.7 Profit before tax (PBT)**

PBT is the profit remaining after accounting for relevant income and expenses, including finance costs, but before income tax.

A simplified relationship is:

PBT = Profit before tax, as reported in the financial statements

Do not assume that subtracting interest and tax from EBITDA gives PAT directly. Depreciation, other income, exceptional items, and other expenses may also affect the result.

## **5.8 Profit after tax (PAT)**

PAT is the profit remaining after income tax.

PAT = Profit before tax − Income tax expense

This is a simplified relationship; reported results can include adjustments and tax-related items.

PAT is often called the **bottom line**.

### **Questions to ask**

- Is PAT growing?
- Is PAT growing faster or slower than revenue?
- Is profit supported by the core business?
- Are unusual gains inflating profit?
- Is the company generating sufficient cash despite reporting profits?

For consolidated results, distinguish profit attributable to the owners of the parent from profit attributable to non-controlling interests.

## **5.9 Notes to accounts**

Never rely only on the main P&L figures.

Investigate the notes for:

- Revenue recognition policies.
- Segment performance.
- Related-party transactions.
- Exceptional items.
- Employee benefit obligations.
- Borrowing costs.
- Contingent liabilities.
- Tax disputes.
- Depreciation policies.
- Significant accounting estimates.

The notes explain what lies behind the reported numbers.

# **6. Balance Sheet**

## **6.1 What is it?**

The Balance Sheet shows the company’s financial position at a specific date.

Unlike the P&L, which covers a period, the Balance Sheet is a snapshot.

For example:

- P&L: performance during FY 2025–26.
- Balance Sheet: financial position as of 31 March 2026.

### **Accounting equation**

**Assets = Liabilities + Equity**

This equation must balance.

## **6.2 Assets**

Assets are economic resources controlled by the company.

They are generally divided into current and non-current assets.

### **A. Non-current assets**

These are assets that do not qualify as current assets.

Examples:

- Property, plant and equipment.
- Long-term investments.
- Intangible assets.
- Patents and trademarks, when recognised under accounting standards.
- Certain long-term financial assets.
- Capital work in progress.

### **What to check**

- Is the company investing in new capacity?
- Are fixed assets increasing?
- Is capital work in progress rising?
- Are investments producing reasonable returns?
- Are intangible assets or goodwill significant?
- Is the company spending heavily on expansion?

Capital investment can support growth, but excessive or unproductive investment can destroy shareholder value.

### **B. Current assets**

Current assets generally include assets expected to be realised, sold, or consumed in the normal operating cycle or within the relevant current-asset classification period.

Examples:

- Inventory.
- Trade receivables.
- Cash and cash equivalents.
- Certain short-term investments.
- Other current assets.

### **What to check**

**Inventory**

- Is inventory increasing faster than sales?
- Could the company be producing more than it can sell?

**Trade receivables**

- Is the company collecting payments from customers?
- Are receivables growing faster than revenue?

**Cash**

- Does the company have enough liquidity?
- Is cash increasing because of healthy operations or additional borrowing?

## **6.3 Total assets**

Total assets = Current assets + Non-current assets

This is a simplified presentation of the asset structure.

A growing asset base can support expansion, but asset growth is not automatically good.

Evaluate whether the assets generate sufficient revenue, profit, and cash.

## **6.4 Liabilities**

Liabilities represent present obligations of the company.

### **A. Non-current liabilities**

These generally represent obligations classified as non-current under the applicable accounting standards.

Examples:

- Long-term borrowings.
- Certain lease liabilities.
- Deferred tax liabilities.
- Certain long-term employee benefit obligations.
- Other long-term obligations.

Investigate:

- Total borrowings.
- Interest rates.
- Repayment schedules.
- Debt maturity profile.
- Whether future cash flows can support repayments.

### **B. Current liabilities**

Examples:

- Short-term borrowings.
- Trade payables.
- Current maturities of long-term debt.
- Certain current lease liabilities.
- Other current obligations.

Current liabilities are generally expected to be settled within the operating cycle or twelve months, depending on the applicable classification rules.

### **Important correction**

Rent is not automatically a non-current liability.

Future rent obligations may be recognised as lease liabilities under applicable accounting standards. The classification depends on the lease and the payment schedule.

## **6.5 Shareholders’ equity**

Equity represents the residual interest in the company’s assets after deducting liabilities.

Equity = Assets − Liabilities

It can include:

- Equity share capital.
- Other equity, including retained earnings and reserves.
- Other relevant equity components.

### **Share capital**

Share capital represents the amount recognised as equity from shares issued by the company.

For example, if a company reports ₹290 crore of share capital, that figure does not mean its current market value is ₹290 crore.

**Share capital is different from market capitalisation.**

Market capitalisation = Current share price × Total outstanding shares

### **Reserves and surplus / other equity**

Retained earnings and other reserves can accumulate from profits, transactions, and other recognised movements.

However, reserves do not increase only because the company earns a profit.

They can also be affected by:

- Dividends.
- Losses.
- Share-based payments.
- Other comprehensive income.
- Corporate actions.
- Other accounting adjustments.

### **Important relationship**

PAT can contribute to retained earnings, but the entire PAT does not necessarily flow into reserves.

Dividends, other transactions, and adjustments affect the final balance.

### **What to check**

- Is equity increasing over time?
- Is debt growing faster than equity?
- Are retained earnings increasing?
- Is the company distributing excessive dividends relative to cash generation?
- Are there large changes in other equity that require explanation?

# **7. Cash Flow Statement**

## **7.1 Why is it important?**

The Cash Flow Statement shows how cash and cash equivalents changed during the reporting period.

It helps answer:

**Is the company generating real cash from its business, and how is that cash being used?**

A company can report accounting profits without generating equivalent operating cash.

## **7.2 Operating activities**

Operating cash flow reflects cash generated or used by the company’s operating activities.

For example, an automobile manufacturer generates operating cash through vehicle sales and pays cash for materials, employees, suppliers, and other operating requirements.

### **What to check**

- Is operating cash flow positive?
- Is it consistently positive across several years?
- Is it broadly keeping pace with PAT over time?
- Is working capital consuming cash?
- Is the company relying on unusual or temporary cash flow improvements?

Positive operating cash flow is generally desirable.

However, examine the reasons behind major year-to-year changes.

## **7.3 Investing activities**

Investing cash flow includes transactions involving long-term assets and investments.

Examples:

- Purchasing plant and machinery.
- Building factories.
- Acquiring businesses.
- Purchasing investments.
- Selling equipment.
- Receiving proceeds from investment disposals.

### **Interpretation**

Negative investing cash flow can indicate that a company is investing in future growth.

Positive investing cash flow can indicate asset sales or investment disposals.

Neither is automatically good or bad.

Investigate the underlying transactions.

**Example:** A company spending ₹500 crore on a new factory may report negative investing cash flow because it is expanding production capacity.

The investment is attractive only if its expected future returns justify the cost.

## **7.4 Financing activities**

Financing cash flow reflects transactions with lenders and shareholders.

Examples:

- Borrowing money.
- Repaying debt.
- Issuing shares.
- Paying dividends.
- Repurchasing shares.
- Certain lease-related payments.

### **Interpretation**

Positive financing cash flow may indicate:

- New borrowings.
- Equity issuance.
- Other financing inflows.

Negative financing cash flow may indicate:

- Debt repayment.
- Dividend payments.
- Share buybacks.
- Other financing outflows.

A company consistently borrowing money to fund operating losses deserves careful investigation.

However, negative financing cash flow can also be a positive sign when a financially strong company is repaying debt or returning surplus cash to shareholders.

## **7.5 Net change in cash**

Net change in cash and cash equivalents = Operating cash flow + Investing cash flow + Financing cash flow

This is a simplified relationship. Foreign exchange effects and other separately presented reconciling items may also affect the total change in cash.

### **Example**

Suppose a company reports:

|**Activity**|**Cash flow**|
|---|---|
|Operating|+₹700 crore|
|Investing|−₹400 crore|
|Financing|−₹200 crore|
|Net change|+₹100 crore|

Interpretation:

- Operations generated ₹700 crore.
- The company invested ₹400 crore.
- It used ₹200 crore for net financing outflows.
- Cash increased by ₹100 crore before any separately presented reconciliation items.

This can be a healthy pattern, provided operating cash flow is sustainable and investments generate appropriate returns.

## **7.6 Free cash flow (FCF)**

Free cash flow is an important metric for investors.

A common simplified formula is:

FCF = Operating cash flow − Capital expenditure

Capital expenditure is often derived from purchases of property, plant and equipment and intangible assets, with adjustments depending on the analysis.

### **Why it matters**

FCF helps investors evaluate the cash available after maintaining or expanding the asset base.

A company can use free cash flow to:

- Repay debt.
- Pay dividends.
- Repurchase shares.
- Fund acquisitions.
- Build cash reserves.

Negative FCF is not automatically bad. A company investing heavily for growth may temporarily generate negative FCF.

The important question is whether the investment is likely to produce attractive future returns.

# **8. Financial Ratios**

Financial ratios help compare a company’s performance across years and against competitors.

Use at least three to five years of historical data where available.

Do not evaluate a company using a single year’s ratios alone.

## **8.1 Profitability ratios**

### **A. Gross profit margin**

Gross profit margin measures how much revenue remains after the relevant direct costs of goods sold.

Gross profit margin = Gross profit / Revenue × 100

For a manufacturer, the calculation depends on how direct production costs are classified.

**Important:** A gross margin of 20% is not a universal minimum for a good company. Compare the business with its own history and industry peers.

### **B. EBITDA margin**

EBITDA margin = EBITDA / Revenue from operations × 100

Example:

- Revenue: ₹1,000 crore.
- EBITDA: ₹150 crore.

EBITDA margin = 150 / 1,000 × 100 = 15%.

Interpretation: The company generates ₹15 of EBITDA for every ₹100 of operating revenue.

A rising margin may indicate better pricing, product mix, scale, or cost management.

Investigate the cause rather than assuming that every increase is sustainable.

### **C. EBIT margin**

EBIT margin = EBIT / Revenue from operations × 100

This includes the effect of depreciation and amortisation.

It can be especially useful when comparing companies with different capital investment requirements.

### **D. PAT margin**

PAT margin = PAT / Revenue from operations × 100

Example:

- Revenue: ₹1,000 crore.
- PAT: ₹100 crore.

PAT margin = 10%.

Interpretation: The company earns ₹10 of net profit for every ₹100 of operating revenue.

If the company has substantial other income or unusual items, examine their effect on PAT.

### **E. Return on equity (ROE)**

ROE measures the return generated on shareholders’ equity.

ROE = PAT attributable to equity shareholders / Average shareholders’ equity × 100

Average equity is generally calculated using opening and closing balances.

Using closing equity alone is a simpler approximation, but average equity is often more informative.

Example:

- PAT: ₹200 crore.
- Average equity: ₹1,000 crore.

ROE = 20%.

Interpretation: The company generated ₹20 in annual net profit for every ₹100 of average shareholders’ equity.

### **Is a higher ROE always better?**

No.

High ROE may reflect:

- Strong profitability.
- Efficient use of capital.
- A business requiring relatively little equity.

But it can also reflect:

- High financial leverage.
- A very small equity base.
- Share buybacks.
- Exceptional or non-recurring profits.

Always evaluate ROE alongside debt, cash flow, and business fundamentals.

### **F. Return on capital employed (ROCE)**

ROCE evaluates how efficiently the business generates operating profit relative to the capital employed.

A common formula is:

ROCE = EBIT / Average capital employed × 100

The exact capital employed calculation should be consistent across the companies being compared.

ROCE is particularly useful for comparing capital-intensive businesses.

---

## **8.2 Leverage and solvency ratios**

These ratios help evaluate the company’s debt burden and financial stability.

### **A. Debt-to-equity ratio**

Debt-to-equity ratio = Total debt / Shareholders’ equity

Example:

- Total debt: ₹500 crore.
- Shareholders’ equity: ₹1,000 crore.

Debt-to-equity = 0.5.

Interpretation: The company has ₹0.50 of debt for every ₹1 of shareholders’ equity.

If debt and equity are equal, the ratio is 1.0.

If debt exceeds equity, the ratio is greater than 1.0.

However, **a ratio below 1 is not automatically safe, and a ratio above 1 is not automatically dangerous**.

Compare the company with its industry and consider its cash generation, interest burden, and debt maturity profile.

Also, be consistent about whether you are measuring total borrowings or net debt.

### **B. Interest coverage ratio**

A common formula is:

Interest coverage ratio = EBIT / Interest expense

Example:

- EBIT: ₹400 crore.
- Interest expense: ₹100 crore.

Interest coverage = 4 times.

Interpretation: EBIT is four times the reported interest expense.

A higher ratio generally indicates a greater ability to cover interest costs.

However, EBIT is not the same as cash available to pay interest. Also examine operating cash flow, debt repayments, and upcoming obligations.

### **C. Net debt**

Net debt = Total debt − Cash and cash equivalents

Some analysts also adjust for certain liquid investments.

Check the company’s cash availability and restrictions before treating all cash as available to repay debt.

A company with more cash than debt may have net cash, but it can still have operational or liquidity risks.

### **D. Current ratio**

Current ratio = Current assets / Current liabilities

This measures short-term balance-sheet coverage.

A higher ratio can indicate a stronger liquidity position, but excessive inventory or slow-moving receivables can inflate it.

Interpret it in the context of the company’s operating cycle and industry.

---

## **8.3 Valuation ratios**

Valuation ratios help assess what investors are paying for a company’s earnings, sales, assets, or cash generation.

They do not independently tell you whether a stock is cheap or expensive.

### **A. Earnings per share (EPS)**

EPS = Profit attributable to equity shareholders / Weighted-average number of ordinary shares

Basic EPS and diluted EPS can differ.

Use diluted EPS when assessing the potential impact of convertible securities and other dilutive instruments.

EPS growth can result from:

- Higher profits.
- A reduction in outstanding shares.
- A combination of both.

Examine the source of growth.

### **B. Price-to-earnings ratio (P/E)**

P/E = Current share price / EPS

Alternatively:

P/E = Market capitalisation / Profit attributable to equity shareholders

These calculations must use consistent earnings and share-count definitions.

Example:

- Share price: ₹1,200.
- EPS: ₹60.

P/E = 20 times.

Interpretation: Investors are paying ₹20 for every ₹1 of annual earnings per share.

A high P/E may reflect strong expected growth, high business quality, or excessive optimism.

A low P/E may indicate undervaluation or genuine business risks.

Compare P/E with:

- Historical valuation.
- Competitors.
- Expected earnings growth.
- Business quality.
- Financial risks.

### **C. Price-to-sales ratio (P/S)**

P/S = Market capitalisation / Revenue

Alternatively:

P/S = Current share price / Sales per share

Where:

Sales per share = Revenue / Relevant number of outstanding shares

Example:

- Market capitalisation: ₹3,500 crore.
- Annual revenue: ₹1,000 crore.

P/S = 3.5 times.

Interpretation: The market values the company at 3.5 times its annual revenue.

**Important:** A P/S ratio of 3.5 does not mean every ₹1 invested earns ₹3.50. It means the market capitalisation is 3.5 times annual revenue.

A high P/S can be justified by strong growth and margins, but it can also indicate overvaluation.

Always examine profitability and cash flow.

### **D. Price-to-book ratio (P/B)**

Book value attributable to ordinary shareholders = Relevant shareholders’ equity attributable to ordinary shareholders

Book value per share = Book value attributable to ordinary shareholders / Outstanding ordinary shares

P/B = Current share price / Book value per share

Alternatively:

P/B = Market capitalisation / Relevant book equity

Use consistent share and equity definitions.

P/B compares the market value of equity with its accounting book value.

A low P/B does not automatically mean a stock is undervalued.

It may indicate:

- Low expected profitability.
- Poor asset quality.
- Business risks.
- A structurally weak business.

A high P/B may be reasonable for businesses that generate high returns on equity, although valuation still matters.

P/B is generally more informative for some financial institutions than for asset-light businesses whose internally generated intangible assets may not be fully reflected on the balance sheet.

# **9. Intrinsic Valuation: Discounted Cash Flow (DCF)**

Intrinsic valuation estimates what a business may be worth based on its expected future cash flows.

DCF is one method for estimating intrinsic value.

## **Basic process**

1. Estimate future free cash flow.
2. Select a suitable forecast period.
3. Estimate a reasonable long-term growth rate.
4. Choose an appropriate discount rate.
5. Calculate the present value of forecast cash flows.
6. Estimate terminal value.
7. Calculate enterprise value.
8. Adjust for net debt and other relevant claims.
9. Estimate equity value per share.

### **Basic principle**

A rupee received in the future is generally worth less than a rupee received today because of the time value of money and investment risk.

### **Important inputs**

- Revenue growth.
- Operating margins.
- Taxes.
- Capital expenditure.
- Working capital requirements.
- Discount rate.
- Terminal growth rate.

### **Important caution**

Small changes in the discount rate or terminal growth rate can significantly change a DCF valuation.

Do not treat a DCF estimate as an exact or guaranteed value.

Use conservative, base-case, and optimistic scenarios.

# **10. Relative Valuation**

Relative valuation compares a company with similar businesses.

For example, when evaluating Bajaj Auto, you might compare it with other relevant listed two-wheeler manufacturers.

Potential comparison metrics include:

- P/E.
- P/B.
- P/S.
- EV/EBITDA.
- ROE.
- ROCE.
- EBITDA margin.
- Revenue growth.
- Debt-to-equity.
- Free cash flow generation.

### **Important rules**

Compare companies with similar:

- Business models.
- Growth prospects.
- Profitability.
- Capital requirements.
- Financial risks.

A company with a higher P/E may deserve a premium if its growth, returns on capital, and business quality are superior.

However, the premium must still be reasonable relative to the expected future performance.

Relative valuation tells you how the market values a company compared with peers. It does not independently establish its intrinsic value.

# **11. My Initial Investment Screening Checklist**

Use this as an initial screening framework, not as a universal set of rules.

## **Business quality**

- I understand the company’s business model.
- The company has identifiable growth opportunities.
- Its competitive position is understandable.
- I have identified the major business risks.
- I have reviewed the MD&A and important disclosures.

## **Growth**

- Revenue has grown over a multi-year period.
- EPS has grown over a multi-year period.
- PAT growth is supported by operating performance.
- I understand the main drivers of future growth.

## **Profitability**

- Gross margin is appropriate for the industry.
- EBITDA margin is stable or improving.
- PAT margin is stable or improving.
- ROE is attractive relative to peers.
- ROCE is attractive relative to peers.

## **Financial stability**

- Debt is manageable.
- Interest coverage is adequate.
- Operating cash flow is sustainable.
- Working capital is under control.
- Debt repayments appear manageable.

## **Cash flow**

- Operating cash flow is positive over a reasonable multi-year period.
- Operating cash flow broadly supports reported profits over time.
- Capital expenditure is understandable.
- I have calculated or reviewed free cash flow.
- Dividends and buybacks are financially sustainable.

## **Valuation**

- I have checked P/E.
- I have checked P/B where relevant.
- I have compared the company with its peers.
- I have considered intrinsic valuation where appropriate.
- My assumptions about future growth are realistic.
- I understand the downside risks if my assumptions are wrong.

# **12. Common Mistakes to Avoid**

1. **Looking only at revenue:** Revenue can rise while profitability and cash generation deteriorate.
2. **Looking only at PAT:** Accounting profit does not guarantee strong operating cash flow.
3. **Treating EBITDA as cash flow:** EBITDA ignores several important cash expenses and investments.
4. **Assuming negative investing cash flow is bad:** It can reflect productive investment in future growth.
5. **Assuming negative financing cash flow is bad:** It can reflect debt repayment or sustainable distributions to shareholders.
6. **Assuming a low P/E means undervaluation:** The market may be pricing in declining earnings or significant risks.
7. **Assuming a high ROE guarantees quality:** Leverage and a small equity base can inflate ROE.
8. **Ignoring subsidiaries:** Standalone statements may not show the full financial performance of a group.
9. **Ignoring the notes to accounts:** Important risks and accounting details can be hidden behind headline numbers.
10. **Using one year’s performance:** Multi-year trends provide a better understanding of the business.
11. **Ignoring valuation:** Even an excellent company can produce poor investment returns if purchased at an excessive price.
12. **Treating screening thresholds as universal rules:** Suitable margins, leverage, growth rates, and valuation multiples vary significantly by industry.

---

# **13. Final Framework**

When analyzing any company, follow this order:

**Business → Management → Financial Statements → Ratios → Valuation → Risks → Investment Decision**

First, understand how the company makes money.

Next, examine its financial performance, balance sheet, and cash generation.

Then, evaluate profitability, debt, and valuation.

Finally, decide whether the expected return justifies the risks and the price you would pay.

The objective is not simply to find a profitable company. It is to identify a financially sound business with sustainable growth potential that is available at a reasonable price.
